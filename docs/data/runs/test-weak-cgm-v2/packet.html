Our assessment: Weak case: this appeal is unlikely to succeed

Better options to try first

Suggestions from the Planner. Unlike the letter, these aren't fact-checked, so confirm products, prices and coverage before relying on them.

  • Over-the-counter CGM: FDA-cleared OTC biosensors for adults with type 2 diabetes not on insulin (for example Dexcom Stelo or Abbott Lingo) are bought without a prescription for roughly $50-100 per month cash. This matches the patient's stated goal of seeing how meals affect glucose.
  • Pay for an OTC CGM with HSA or FSA funds if the patient has an account; glucose monitors are generally eligible medical expenses.
  • Ask the provider about a short-term professional (clinic-owned) CGM study, billed under CPT 95249/95250/95251, if the plan covers diagnostic CGM separately from personal DME. Confirm coverage before using it.
  • Use covered blood glucose meter and test strip benefits for structured paired fingerstick testing (before and 2 hours after meals) to learn meal responses at no extra coverage risk.
  • Use covered diabetes self-management education (DSMES) and medical nutrition therapy benefits for meal-planning and weight-loss support.
  • Check plan wellness, disease-management, or digital diabetes programs, and manufacturer discount or trial programs for CGMs.
  • If the patient's clinical status truly changes later (insulin started or documented level 2/3 hypoglycemia), submit a new prior authorization rather than appealing this denial.
  • If the patient still wants to appeal after being told the merit is weak, an internal appeal and then external review remain available. Expectations should be set realistically.

Needs your attention

  • Reviewer still expects the insurer to argue: Criterion I.B is mandatory and conjunctive with I.A–I.E, and the member concedes it is not met. MP-DME-031 Section III states CGM is not medically necessary when Section I is not met. Appeal reviewers apply the policy as written and have no authority to rewrite I.B on an individual appeal, so the denial is upheld on the member's own admission.
  • Reviewer still expects the insurer to argue: The guideline-alignment argument fails. The 01/01/2026 revision postdates publication of the 2026 ADA Standards, which are released each December. The plan therefore had them in view and deliberately limited coverage to insulin use or problematic hypoglycemia. The 'any diabetes treatment where CGM helps' recommendation is Grade C, the lowest evidence grade, and the plan is not obligated to adopt every society recommendation.
  • Reviewer still expects the insurer to argue: The member's A1c is 6.6%, already at goal and improving on metformin alone, with no hypoglycemia. A pooled 0.31-point A1c reduction is of marginal clinical value for a well-controlled patient, and nothing in the record shows CGM would change management. The plan is continue metformin and recheck in 6 months.
  • Reviewer still expects the insurer to argue: The progress note records the purpose as the patient's curiosity about meal effects and weight loss, ordered 'at patient request.' The letter mentions only the meal-effects reason and omits weight loss. Convenience, lifestyle and weight-loss monitoring are not medical necessity, and the selective summary undermines credibility.
  • Reviewer still expects the insurer to argue: Even if I.B were waived, I.C (FDA indication/device), I.D (training) and the I.E determination that criteria A–D are met are undocumented. The prescriber never documented a medical-necessity determination. Independent grounds to deny remain.

1. Current professional guidelines recommend CGM beyond insulin users

The American Diabetes Association’s Standards of Care in Diabetes—2026 include Recommendation 7.15, which is new in the 2026 Standards. It recommends CGM from diabetes onset onward for adults on insulin (Grade A), on noninsulin therapies that can cause hypoglycemia (Grade C), and on any diabetes treatment where CGM helps in management (Grade C). The ADA states that the choice of device and method should reflect the individual’s circumstances, preferences, and needs. Its supporting text cites a meta-analysis backing CGM in non-insulin type 2 diabetes, with better glycemic outcomes, reduced health care resource use, and acceptable cost-effectiveness.

MP-DME-031’s own revision history states that criterion I.B was revised on 01/01/2026 “to align with updated national coverage criteria and professional society guidelines.” Because Recommendation 7.15 is new in the 2026 Standards, I respectfully ask the plan’s medical reviewers to consider whether criterion I.B reflects it, and to evaluate my request in light of it.

2. How my case relates to MP-DME-031

The policy covers CGM when “ALL of the following criteria are met.” I want to be candid about where I stand:

  • I.A – Diagnosis of diabetes mellitus: Met. I have type 2 diabetes mellitus (E11.9), diagnosed in 2024.
  • I.B – Insulin-treated or documented problematic hypoglycemia: Not met as currently written. I take metformin only. I am not on insulin or a sulfonylurea, and I have never had a glucose reading below 70 mg/dL. I am not asking the plan to find otherwise. I am asking it to reconsider in light of the 2026 ADA recommendation described above.
  • I.E – Recent practitioner visit: My treating practitioner evaluated my diabetes control at a visit on 09/22/2026, including a hemoglobin A1c, and the CGM was ordered at that visit.

4. Supporting evidence in non-insulin type 2 diabetes

  • A 2026 review reports a meta-analysis limited to non-insulin patients in which CGM was associated with a 0.31 percentage-point HbA1c reduction and an 8.63 percentage-point increase in time in range. The review notes that CGM may help support lifestyle discussions, and that the evidence is stronger for glycemic metrics than for long-term outcomes.

  • The ADA Standards (2025) describe professional CGM as a clinic-placed device worn for a discrete period, generally 7–14 days. They note that some data show benefit from intermittent CGM use in type 2 diabetes on noninsulin therapies. This supports my alternative request above.

  • EV-3 LCD L33822: Glucose Monitors (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services): Medicare covers CGM when it is prescribed to improve glycemic control and the patient either uses insulin or has documented problematic hypoglycemia. Problematic hypoglycemia means recurrent level 2 events (<54 mg/dL) that persist despite treatment changes, or one level 3 event requiring third-party assistance.
  • EV-4 Glucose Monitoring Supplies (Medicare Provider Compliance Tips) (CMS Medicare Learning Network): Restates the Medicare CGM criteria: a prescription to improve glycemic control plus either insulin treatment or documented problematic hypoglycemia (level 2 or level 3 events).
  • EV-5 Continuous glucose monitoring in adults with type 2 diabetes: a systematic review and meta-analysis (Jancev et al.) (Diabetologia (via PMC)): Across adults with type 2 diabetes, whether or not they used insulin, CGM produced a modest HbA1c reduction of about 0.31% (3.43 mmol/mol).
  • EV-6 CGM Beyond Intensive Insulin Therapy in Type 2 Diabetes (PMC (2026 review)): Reports a meta-analysis limited to non-insulin patients in which CGM was associated with a 0.31 percentage point HbA1c reduction and an 8.63 percentage point increase in time in range. CGM may help support lifestyle discussions, but the evidence is stronger for glycemic metrics than for long-term outcomes.
  • EV-7 Ida et al., 2019 meta-analysis of CGM in type 2 diabetes (PMC): A meta-analysis of 7 trials (669 patients) found no difference in body weight or blood pressure between the CGM and comparison groups.
  • EV-8 Systematic review of real-time CGM trials (PMC): Non-insulin-treated groups had smaller HbA1c changes than insulin-treated groups but showed improved glycemic variability. Treatment satisfaction was consistently higher with real-time CGM.
  • EV-9 FDA Clears First Over-the-Counter Continuous Glucose Monitor (U.S. Food and Drug Administration): An OTC CGM (Stelo) was cleared in March 2024 for adults 18 and older who do not use insulin, such as people treating diabetes with oral medications. It is not intended for people with problematic hypoglycemia.
  • EV-10 FDA 510(k) clearance letter K233861 (Libre Rio) (U.S. Food and Drug Administration): Cleared as an OTC integrated CGM for adults 18 and older who do not use insulin.
  • EV-11 FDA 510(k) clearance letter K233655 (Lingo) (U.S. Food and Drug Administration): Cleared for people 18 and older who are not on insulin.
  • EV-12 7. Diabetes Technology: Standards of Care in Diabetes—2025 (American Diabetes Association, Diabetes Care 48(Suppl 1):S146): Defines professional CGM as a device placed in the clinician's office and worn for a discrete period, generally 7–14 days.

Why the plan’s criteria come first: an appeal is decided against the plan’s terms and clinical criteria, with a fresh look by a reviewer who doesn’t defer to the first decision (29 CFR 2560.503-1(h)(3)), and external review asks whether the denial was correct under the plan and the medical evidence (45 CFR 147.136). The three judgments: the Planner rates merit from the case facts before any research (strong: the record clearly meets the criteria or the denial plainly misapplies them; possible: contestable judgment or missing documents; weak: the denial correctly applies the terms); the Policy Analyst marks each plan criterion met, not met or unclear with its source (strong: every required criterion met or the policy misapplied; possible: one unclear; weak: one clearly not met); the Adversarial Reviewer plays the insurer’s appeals medical director and scores the final draft 1–10, approving only at 8/10 or higher with no required changes, and must be tough but fair. Code, not a model, combines them: weak if the Reviewer scores 3/10 or lower or the Planner or Policy Analyst says weak; strong only if both say strong and the Reviewer scores 8/10 or higher; possible otherwise. The asymmetry is deliberate: a false “good chance” costs a sick person time and money. The app shows the verdicts as Good chance, Worth trying and Unlikely to succeed. Tested: cgm-denial (Rosa) strong, approved at 9/10, every claim verified; weak-cgm weak, all three agreed (Reviewer 2/10) and revising stopped after round 1, with alternatives listed. Limits: the thresholds (8/10 approve, 3/10 stop) are design choices, not calibrated against real outcomes; four synthetic cases only; ratings are model judgment and only as good as the documents (a true but undocumented event can make the verdict too pessimistic, so the packet says what to ask the doctor to document); plan types differ (Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, self-funded plans).
